Car Buying Basics

Yes...it is that expensive

Can we be real for a second?buying a car can feel like a mix of excitement and pure stress. One minute you’re imagining yourself cruising in your new ride, and the next you’re drowning in prices, loan terms, and dealership jargon. It’s a lot. But here’s the truth: car buying doesn’t have to be scary or confusing. Once you understand the basics, you’ll feel confident making the choice that’s best for you, not the dealership.

New vs. Used Cars: What’s the Difference?

When you begin your car shopping journey, you'll quickly realize you have two main paths: New or Used. Both come with pros and cons, but neither is a "better" choice. It all depends on your budget, lifestyle, and comfort level.

New Cars

  • Higher price
  • Comes with full warranties
  • No previous owners
  • Latest features and technology
  • Depreciates quickly (lose value fast)

Used Cars

  • Lower price
  • Shorter or limited warranties
  • Has previous owners
  • Can be more affordable
  • Slower depreciation (doesn't lose value as fast)

Understanding Car Pricing

Car pricing can be more complicated than just the sticker on the window. Here are the main terms you’ll see:

  • MSRP (Manufacturer’s Suggested Retail Price)
    • The price the car company recommends. Think of it as the “starting point.”
  • Dealer Price
    • What the dealership actually paid for the car. This is usually lower than MSRP.
  • Out‑the‑Door Price (OTD)
    • The real price you pay -- including taxes, fees, and add‑ons. This is the number you should focus on.
  • Depreciation
    • Cars lose value over time. This drop in value doesn’t mean anything is “wrong” with the car; it’s simply how the market works.
    • Example:
      • A car purchased brand‑new for $30,000 may only be worth $24,000–$25,000 the moment it’s driven off the lot.
      • After one year, that same car might be valued around $21,000–$23,000, even if it’s in great condition.
      • After three years, the value may drop closer to $15,000–$18,000, depending on mileage and wear.

What's the Best Way to Purchase a Car?

This is one of the rare times where we'll give you a specific answer: Cash. This may seem old school, but consumers usually have more to lose when financing a depreciating asset like a car. However, we understand that financing may be easier for some situations, so here is what you need to know when looking at financing options:

  • Loan Amount
    • The total you’re borrowing after your down payment.
  • Interest Rate
    • The cost of borrowing money. Lower is better.
  • Loan Term
    • How long you’ll be paying the loan (usually 36–72 months).
    • Longer terms = lower monthly payments but more interest overall.
  • Monthly Payment
    • What you owe each month. Make sure this fits your budget comfortably.
  • Down Payment
    • Money you pay upfront. A larger down payment reduces your loan amount and interest.

TIP: Your local credit union may offer better interest rates for financing than dealerhip banking.

Car buying is a big purchase, but it becomes a lot less intimidating once you understand how pricing, depreciation, and financing really work. The goal isn’t to find the “perfect” car, it’s to choose one that fits your budget, your needs, and your peace of mind. When you stay informed and intentional, you walk away with a car that supports your life instead of stretching it.
