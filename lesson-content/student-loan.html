Student Loans

The Bane of every college student's existance

Let’s admit it, college can be very expensive. While having grants and scholarships cover all four years is the dream, it’s not always realistic. The truth is, many students will need to take on student loan debt. Before you panic, take a breath and read this clearly: taking on student debt is not the end of the world. What is important is understanding what student loans are, how they work, and how they can impact your future goals.

What Types of Student Loans Are There?

There are two main types of student loans: Federal and Private. Federal loans are offered by the government and usually come with lower interest rates, flexible repayment options and borrower protections. Private loans (also known as personal student loans) come from banks or lenders and often cost more. For this lesson, we will be focusing on federal student loans.

What Are Federal Student Loans?

Federal student loans are borrowed money used to pay for college expenses like tuition, housing, books, and fees. They differ from everyday personal loans in a few important ways:

  1. No set loan term - You typically begin repayment six months after graduation with a repayment period between 10 - 30 years.
  2. No credit check required - Federal loans are awarded through your school’s financial aid package. The amount you receive is based on your FAFSA information, which looks at your (and/or your family’s) income — not your credit score.

Easy enough right! Before we get too ahead of ourselves, its important to understand that this isn’t “free” money the government is handing you (that would be a grant or scholarship). Student loans always come with a cost we call interest (more on that in the next section).

Types of Federal Student Loans

There are 2 types of federal student loans you are most likely to receive in your financial aid package:

  • Direct Subsidized
    • Eligible for undergraduate students with financial need
    • The government pays your interest while you’re in school (meaning no interest is accrued while you are working towards your degree)
  • Direct Unsubsidized
    • Eligible for undergraduate and graduate students
    • Interest starts accruing immediately
    • No financial need is required

The key difference between the two loans is how interest impacts the overall balance. Interest is the extra amount you pay back (the cost) in addition to the money you originally borrowed. That cost continues to grow over time at a set percentage rate. As long as the loan remains active, interest will continue to build.
